Three bot archetypes: why one eval template cannot serve all three

A chatbot, a text-to-query bot and an analytics bot look like three instances of the same thing on an org chart. They are not. They differ in whether ground truth exists at all, which means they differ in what can be measured, what counts as good, how they fail, and what has to be true before they ship. This is the session where you stop asking "how accurate is it" and start asking "accurate at what, graded how, against whose bar".

Part 0

The one distinction everything else follows from

Ask of any bot: can a machine tell whether the answer was right, without asking a person? For a text-to-query bot, yes - run the query, compare the rows. For a support chatbot, no - correctness is an opinion until a human or a calibrated judge supplies one. For an analytics bot, half and half, and the half that cannot be checked sits on top of the half that can. Everything on this page is a consequence of that one question.

Part 1 · the one slide

Five axes, three answers, no overlap 8 min live

This is the slide to carry into every launch review for the next year. If your organisation has one eval checklist, it was written for one of these three columns and it is blind to the other two.

LiveWhy 40-70% is a good support-bot number and 99% is a definition problem4 min▶

Resolution rate has no natural definition, so the number moves enormously with the choice - which makes it the single most gameable figure in this whole course. Intercom publishes theirs, and reading it is instructive precisely because most vendors do not:

  • Hard resolution means the customer explicitly confirmed they were helped. Soft resolution means the customer left and did not come back within 24 hours.
  • A soft resolution is reversed if the customer returns to the same thread. A greeting-only conversation never counts at all.
  • Across their reported base of 7,000+ customers and 40M+ conversations, production resolution runs 38-72% with an average around 67%. That average is a secondary claim reaching us through third parties, so treat the range as the more useful figure.

Separately, containment rate - the share of conversations never handed to a human - is commonly cited as 20-40% early, 70-90% at maturity, with a well-configured RAG chatbot around 40-65%. Those figures are Gartner-attributed and also secondary. Both metrics point the same way: somewhere between four and seven in ten is what a working support bot looks like. When somebody presents 99%, they have not built a better bot; they have counted greetings, or they have counted "the bot replied" as a resolution.

Self-studyThe analytics column is the other two stacked3 min read▶

An analytics bot runs a query and then writes a sentence about the result. The query half has executable ground truth. The sentence half does not. Errors compound across the join: a correct number described wrongly and a wrong number described convincingly both leave the building as an insight.

The published cascade for chart generation makes this concrete. In Microsoft's VisEval work, GPT-4 produced invalid code 3.29% of the time with matplotlib and 25.41% with seaborn - so validity itself depends on which library you asked for. Past validity comes legality: an illegal rate of 21.44% even on matplotlib, meaning code that runs cleanly and produces a chart that does not answer the question asked. Overall pass rate landed at 75.27% for GPT-4, 61.79% for GPT-3.5 and 51.59% for Gemini-Pro, with readability around 3.80 out of 5. A chart that renders is not a chart that is right, and a chart that is right is not an insight that follows.

Part 2 · the ceiling

Why 80-90% is the honest text-to-query number 8 min live

Of the three archetypes this one has real, machine-checkable ground truth, which should make it the easy case. It is instead the archetype where the loudest accuracy claims are made, because a runnable answer is easy to score and easy to score generously. Three independent pieces of evidence converge on the same ceiling.

Real world

The Snowflake disclosure is the most useful of the three, and it is the one people skip. A vendor reporting 90%+ could have stopped at the headline. Instead the method is published: an internal 150-question benchmark, a hand-built semantic model over the schema, agentic retries, and lenient scoring that accepts more than one correct answer. The same blog reports that single-shot GPT-4o managed 51% on that identical set. Read those two numbers together and you learn the thing that matters for your own project - most of the distance between 51% and 90% was bought with engineering around the model and generosity in the grader, not with the model itself. That is a genuinely encouraging finding, and it is also the reason you cannot take 90% into your own launch review as an expectation. It is internal and not independently verified; say so when you cite it.

Why schema linking is the failure to ask about When a text-to-query bot is wrong, it is usually not because it wrote bad SQL. It is because it picked the wrong table or the wrong column - 37% of BIRD errors by one analysis, with JOIN errors adding another 36%, and above 60% in large multi-database settings. That failure is invisible in the output: the query runs, returns rows, and the rows are wrong. It is also the failure your data model can fix without touching the model at all, which makes it the cheapest lever available to you as a leader. Session b6 measures it.
Part 3 · the running case

Meet the three Beacon bots 5 min live

Beacon is shipping all three archetypes this quarter, out of one engineering organisation, under one set of standards. Every remaining session in this course returns to these three, and the contrast between them is the spine of the whole thing.

The counter-intuitive line to remember Beacon's support bot will report the lowest number of the three and carry the highest bar. That is not a contradiction - accuracy and stakes are set by different things. Accuracy is set by whether ground truth exists; the bar is set by what happens when the answer is wrong. Any framework that ties them together will either over-gate the analyst bot or under-gate the support bot, and in practice it does both at once.
Decision 1 of 2

Classify your own portfolio ★ 8 min · everyone writes

List every bot, assistant or agent live or planned in your organisation. Then run each one down the five axes. The exercise takes eight minutes and usually produces two surprises.

Assign an archetype to each using only the ground-truth question: can a machine tell whether the answer was right without asking a person? Yes means text-to-query. No means chatbot. Partly means analytics, and partly is more common than people expect.

Write down the headline metric each one currently reports. Then check it against the second row of the table. A support bot reporting execution accuracy, or a query bot reporting a judge score, has borrowed the wrong template - and that borrowing is almost always invisible until you write the two side by side.

Write down what each one currently claims as its accuracy. Compare against row three. Anything meaningfully above the realistic band is a definition question, not an achievement, and you now know exactly which definition to ask about.

Circle the unique gate each one is missing. Most organisations run one gate - usually a quality number - across the whole portfolio. The safety red team, the read-only rail and the reproducibility check tend to be missing entirely, because they belong to only one column each.

Find the mismatch. Two surprises show up reliably: a bot everyone calls a chatbot that is really a text-to-query bot with a chat window on it, and a bot everyone calls an analytics tool that is generating narrative nobody is grading. Name both out loud.

The hybrid nobody classifies A support bot that can look up your order status is two archetypes in one conversation: a chatbot wrapped around a query. The retrieval half has executable ground truth and should be graded there; the conversational half does not and cannot. Teams grade the whole thing with a judge because the outside is a chat window - and throw away the cheapest, most truthful signal they have. Ask where the executable half of your chatbot is.
Decision 2 of 2

The 96% in the room ★ 7 min · everyone discusses

A vendor presents a text-to-query product at 96% accuracy. Your own team's internal prototype is at 74% and everybody in the room has just become quiet. Work out, in pairs, what you would ask - then compare against the list below.

★ The four questions, in the order that saves the most time

1 · On whose data? A published leaderboard number, an internal set, or your schema? Enterprise workflows crater results: 17.0% on Spider 2.0 against 91.2% on the older, cleaner Spider 1.0, from the same class of model.

2 · Who wrote the correct answers, and were they audited? Gold-annotation error rates of 52.8% and 62.8% have been measured on two widely used splits, and correcting them shifted rankings by up to nine positions. A number scored against unaudited gold answers is a number with a wide unlabelled error bar.

3 · What was added around the model? A semantic layer, retries, multi-gold lenient scoring - all legitimate engineering, and all of it yours to build and maintain. Single-shot with none of it can be half the headline figure.

4 · What does it do when it does not know? A model with no abstention path can score worse than useless once you penalise confident wrong answers, which is exactly what a reliability-scored benchmark does. Ask to see a question it refuses.

What you are actually deciding Not whether 96% is true - it may well be, on the set it was measured on. You are deciding whether that measurement transfers to your schema, your questions and your grader. The honest answer is usually "some of it does", and the four questions tell you which part. The version of this conversation that goes badly is the one where nobody asks, the number is believed, and the gap is discovered in production by an analyst who trusted a plausible-looking figure.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 40-50 min total

  • Finish the portfolio classification for every bot in your organisation, including the ones in other teams. Send the table round. The value is less in the classification than in the argument it starts about which metric each one should be reporting.
  • For your customer-facing bot, get the written definition of resolution or containment in your own words from whoever owns the number. If nobody can produce one, you have found your first real finding of this course, and it is a bigger one than any accuracy figure.
  • For your text-to-query bot, ask which questions in the eval set were written by the same person who built the schema. Then ask what happens when a question needs a JOIN across two domains nobody owns.
  • Take one accuracy claim you currently believe - vendor or internal - and run the four questions from Decision 2 against it. Write down which of the four you cannot answer. That list is what you take into session a3, which is entirely about reading eval reports.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A team reports 99% resolution on your customer support bot. What have you most likely been shown?

Resolution has no natural definition, which makes it the most gameable number in the course. Intercom's published rules are worth borrowing precisely because they are strict: hard resolution needs explicit confirmation, soft resolution needs the customer to stay away for 24 hours, a soft resolution is reversed if they come back, and greeting-only conversations never count. Under rules like those, production runs 38-72%. Ask for the definition before you argue about the number.

2 · Why is 80-90% treated as the honest ceiling for text-to-query accuracy rather than a target to be beaten?

The ceiling is empirical, not theoretical. Each of the three pieces would be arguable alone; together they say the same thing from a benchmark's own human baseline, from an independent audit of the answer key, and from a vendor's own account of its method. Note what the third one actually shows: most of the distance from 51% to 90% was bought with engineering around the model and generosity in the grader, and both of those are things you would have to build and maintain yourself.

3 · Your organisation has one eval checklist used for all three bots. What goes wrong?

The dangerous outcome is not a false alarm, it is a silent pass. Every axis differs: ground truth exists for one and not the others, the headline metric is different, the honest band for good is different, the signature failure is different, and each has a unique gate the others do not need. A checklist built around resolution rate will never ask a text-to-query bot whether it can refuse, and will never ask an analytics bot whether the same question twice gives the same number.
